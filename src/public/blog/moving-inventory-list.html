Moving Inventory List: Track Every Box From Old Home to New

By the BinTrove team · November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

The worst part of moving isn't the lifting — it's the not knowing. Which box has the coffee maker? Did everything actually make it off the truck? Why is the router in a box labeled "misc"? A moving inventory list answers all of that. It takes a few extra seconds per box while you pack, and it turns moving day and the week after into something you can actually stay on top of. Here's how to build one.

Number every box

Don't write a paragraph on the side of each box — write a number. Box 1, Box 2, Box 3. The number is the handle; everything else about the box lives on your list, tied to that number. This is faster to write, faster to read across a room, and it's what lets you count boxes instead of reading them.

Record contents and the destination room

For each number, jot two things: roughly what's inside, and which room it goes to in the new home. "Box 14 — kitchen — pots, mixing bowls, coffee maker." Now your movers (or your friends with pizza) can carry each box straight to the right room, and you're not restacking everything the next morning. Add the room to the box label too, big enough to read from the doorway.

Photograph the contents

Before you tape a box shut, snap a photo of what's inside. It's faster than writing every item, it's far more complete, and it does double duty: when you're hunting for the phone chargers on night one, you search instead of unpacking five boxes — and if a mover damages something, that photo is your proof of what was in the box and its condition before it went on the truck.

Pack an "open first" box. Chargers, toiletries, a few dishes, coffee, bedding, basic tools. Give it an obvious number and flag it at the top of your list so it's the first thing off the truck.

Flag fragile and high-value boxes

Mark on your list which boxes are fragile and which hold anything valuable — electronics, art, the good dishes. That tells everyone which boxes to load last and carry carefully, and if you've bought moving insurance or coverage through the mover, your photographed, flagged inventory is exactly the documentation a claim needs.

Check boxes off — onto the truck and off again

This is the step that saves a move. As boxes go onto the truck, count them against your list. As they come off at the new home, check each number back in. If Box 23 never gets checked off, you know while the movers are still there — not three weeks later when you finally notice the winter coats never turned up. A numbered list turns "I think everything made it" into a certainty.

Frequently asked questions

What should a moving inventory list include?

For each box: a unique number, what's inside, the destination room, and whether it's fragile or valuable. A photo of the contents beats a written list and doubles as proof of condition. The list lets you check every box off the truck and spot a missing one instantly.

How do I make sure nothing gets lost in a move?

Number every box and keep a list. Count boxes onto the truck, then count them off at the new place — a missing number shows up right away, while the movers are still there.

Should I photograph my boxes when moving?

Yes — a photo of each open box captures contents faster than writing them, helps you find things before unpacking everything, and is your evidence if something is damaged in transit.
