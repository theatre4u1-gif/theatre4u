Home Inventory

How to Make a Home Inventory for Insurance (the Fast, Photo-First Way)

By the BinTrove team · November 25, 2026 · 7 min read

Here's an uncomfortable exercise: if your home were damaged by fire, flood, or theft tomorrow, could you sit down and list everything you lost — every appliance, every tool, the contents of every closet — with enough detail to file a claim? Almost nobody can, which is why claims get underpaid. A home inventory fixes that, and the good news is it's a photo job, not a spreadsheet job. You can get the bulk of it done in an afternoon.

Why the day you need it is the worst day to start

After a loss, you're stressed, possibly displaced, and working from memory. Insurers ask for an itemized list of what was damaged, and anything you can't remember or prove, you generally don't get reimbursed for. People routinely forget a third of what they owned — the stuff in drawers, the tools in the garage, the bins in the attic. A home inventory you built calmly, ahead of time, turns that memory test into a lookup.

What to actually capture

You don't need a forensic record of every fork. Match the effort to the value:

  • Everyday belongings: a clear photo and a short description is plenty. The photo proves you owned it and shows its condition.
  • Higher-value items (electronics, appliances, jewelry, tools, instruments): add the brand or model, roughly what you paid, and when you bought it. Photograph the serial number and any receipt you still have.
  • Collections and sets: note the quantity — "12 place settings," "40+ power tools" — so a pile in a photo translates into a number on a claim.
The single highest-value habit: photograph serial numbers and receipts at purchase. A picture of the box label the day the TV arrives is worth more than an hour of hunting for it later.

The room-by-room method that takes an afternoon

  1. Work one room at a time. Pick a corner and move around the room so you don't double-back or lose your place.
  2. Take a wide shot, then close-ups. One photo of the whole room captures dozens of items at once. Then zoom in on anything valuable.
  3. Open the hidden spots. Closets, drawers, cabinets, the pantry. That's where the forgettable-but-expensive things live.
  4. Add detail only where it pays. Tag the expensive items with brand, price, and date as you go. Leave the rest as photos.
  5. Do the storage, too. The garage, attic, and closet bins hold real money — holiday decor, power tools, seasonal gear. Inventory those bins like any other room.

Photos beat lists — here's why

A written list says "blender." A photo shows which blender, what condition it was in, and that it sat on your counter. Adjusters accept photos as proof of ownership and condition, and a photo captures everything in frame in one tap, so you move through a house far faster than typing item names. Lead with pictures; reserve typing for the handful of things where brand, model, and price actually move the claim.

Keep it somewhere a fire can't reach

An inventory saved only on the laptop in your home office is gone in the exact event you built it for. Keep it off your device — in the cloud, or an app that syncs — so you can open it from a phone at a hotel the night after a loss. "Off-site and reachable from anywhere" is the whole point.

Update it twice a year (it takes minutes)

An inventory drifts the moment you buy a new couch or sell the old bikes. You don't need a full redo — just add big purchases as they happen, and do a quick walk-through when you're already decorating for the holidays or doing spring cleaning. Twice a year keeps it claim-ready.

Frequently asked questions

What should a home inventory include?

For most belongings, a clear photo and a short description is enough. For higher-value items, add the brand or model, roughly what you paid, the purchase date, and a photo of the serial number or receipt. The goal is to prove what you owned and its condition.

How do I make a home inventory fast?

Go room by room and photograph everything — a wide shot plus close-ups of valuables. Photos capture dozens of items at once, so a whole house takes an afternoon. Add written detail only for the expensive things.

Do I need receipts for everything?

No. Receipts help for high-value items, but a photo of the item, its condition, and its serial number goes a long way for the rest. Attach receipts where you still have them.

Where should I keep it?

Somewhere that survives the event you're insuring against — the cloud or an app that syncs off your device, not a file saved only on a home computer.
